Getting Started

Hindi for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Hindi is the most widely spoken language in India and one of the most spoken languages on earth — over 340 million people speak it as their first language, and well over 600 million can use it in total, from Delhi and Mumbai to Fiji, Mauritius and a worldwide diaspora. It is also the language of Bollywood, so even people who have never studied it have been humming its words for years.

A language built for conversation

Spoken Hindi is warm, rhythmic and forgiving. It shares its everyday spoken form with Urdu — together often called Hindustani — which means the Hindi you learn here will be understood in markets and taxis across North India and Pakistan too. Words flow into each other, questions rise at the end of the sentence, and a single greeting, नमस्ते (namaste), opens almost every interaction with folded hands and good wishes built in.

How Hindi sentences are built

English puts the verb in the middle: "I read a book." Hindi puts it at the end: मैं किताब पढ़ता हूँ (main kitaab padhta hoon — literally "I book read"). Hindi also uses postpositions instead of prepositions — the little word comes after the noun, so "in the house" is घर में (ghar mein, "house in"). Once your ear accepts these two habits, Hindi sentences become predictable. Our sentence guide explains the whole system calmly.

Three words for "you"

The one thing to know early: Hindi has three levels of "you" — आप (aap, respectful), तुम (tum, friendly) and तू (tu, very intimate or rude). Beginners should use aap with everyone; it is never wrong, and Indians appreciate the politeness. Verbs change to match, which is why आप कैसे हैं? (aap kaise hain? — how are you?) is the safe form to memorise first.

The first words to learn

Start with survival words: नमस्ते (namaste — hello), धन्यवाद (dhanyavaad — thank you), पानी (paani — water), खाना (khaana — food), कितने का है? (kitne ka hai? — how much is it?) and कहाँ है? (kahaan hai? — where is it?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already handle rickshaws, tea stalls and bazaars. Our everyday vocabulary guide gives you sixty of the most useful words in one place.

Learning the script

Hindi is written in Devanagari, the script with the famous headline running across the top of every word. It looks intricate, but it is one of the most logical writing systems in the world: each letter has one sound, and words are spelled exactly as they are spoken. Most learners can read shop signs within a few weeks of short daily practice. Our Devanagari guide walks through the whole system step by step.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on script, five on new words, and five speaking aloud — even to yourself. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your job, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones, and Bollywood songs suddenly become free listening practice.

Above all, speak from the first week. A single नमस्ते at a Delhi chai stall will teach you more about Indian hospitality than a chapter of grammar. Dhanyavaad — and welcome to the language.

The Script

The Devanagari Script Explained: Vowels, Consonants and the Headline

Devanagari is the script of Hindi, Marathi, Nepali and Sanskrit — and it is one of the most scientific writing systems ever designed. Its letters hang from a line rather than sitting on one, each symbol stands for exactly one sound, and the consonants are arranged in a grid by where in the mouth they are produced. Learn the logic once and you can pronounce any Hindi word you can read.

The headline: shirorekha

The horizontal line across the top of almost every letter is called the शिरोरेखा (shirorekha — "head line"). Letters hang below it like clothes on a washing line: नमस्ते (namaste) is really four letter-shapes strung along one continuous stroke. When you write Hindi by hand, you draw the letters first and add the headline last, in one sweep per word.

The vowels (स्वर)

Hindi has eleven main vowels: अ a, आ aa, इ i, ई ee, उ u, ऊ oo, ऋ ri, ए e, ऐ ai, ओ o and औ au. Two signs complete the set: अं adds a nasal hum (the dot is called anusvara) and अः adds a soft breath. Short and long pairs matter — दिन (din — day) and दीन (deen — poor, humble) differ only in vowel length.

The consonants (व्यंजन): a grid of the mouth

The 33 consonants are arranged in five rows of five, ordered from the back of the throat to the lips. The throat row: क ka, ख kha, ग ga, घ gha, ङ nga. Then the palate row च cha, छ chha, ज ja, झ jha, ञ nya; the tongue-tip row ट ta, ठ tha, ड da, ढ dha, ण na; the teeth row त ta, थ tha, द da, ध dha, न na; and the lip row प pa, फ pha, ब ba, भ bha, म ma. The pattern in each row is identical: plain, breathed, voiced, voiced-and-breathed, nasal. After the grid come य ya, र ra, ल la, व va and the sibilants श sha, ष sha, स sa, plus ह ha.

Matras: vowels in shorthand

Inside a word, vowels are written as small signs called matras that attach to consonants: का kaa, कि ki (written before the letter but read after it), की kee, कु ku, कू koo, के ke, कै kai, को ko, कौ kau. A bare consonant already contains the vowel "a", and a slanted stroke called halant (क्) cancels it. This is why Hindi spelling is so regular: what you see is exactly what you say.

Conjuncts: when letters merge

When two consonants meet without a vowel between them, they fuse into a conjunct. Most are simply the first letter cut in half, but three famous special forms must be memorised: क्ष (ksha, in क्षत्रिय), त्र (tra, in मित्र — mitra, friend) and ज्ञ (gya, in ज्ञान — gyaan, knowledge). You will also see dotted letters for sounds borrowed from Persian, Arabic and English: क़ qa, ख़ kha, ग़ ga, ज़ za (as in ज़रूर — zaroor, certainly) and फ़ fa.

Devanagari digits

Hindi has its own numerals: ० १ २ ३ ४ ५ ६ ७ ८ ९ for 0 to 9. You will see them on older signs, calendars and government documents, though everyday prices and phone numbers use the familiar international digits. Our numbers guide teaches the number words themselves.

Learn the vowels first, then one consonant row a day, reading real things — bus boards, shop names, film posters — as you go. Within a month, Devanagari stops being decoration and starts being information. Pair this guide with our beginners guide and you have the complete foundation.

How to Translate English to Hindi Accurately

Machine translation between English and Hindi has improved enormously, but the two languages are built very differently — Hindi puts the verb last, marks respect in every sentence, and gives every noun a gender. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often.

1. Decide who "you" is: aap, tum or tu

English has one "you"; Hindi has three. आप (aap) is respectful — for elders, officials, strangers and customers. तुम (tum) is friendly — for friends and younger people. तू (tu) is extremely intimate and can sound rude. A translator usually defaults to aap, which is the safe choice. If your message is to a close friend and the output feels stiff, that is why — and knowing the reason lets you adjust a word or two by hand.

2. Remember the speaker's gender

Hindi verbs reveal the speaker's gender: a man says मैं जाता हूँ (main jaata hoon — I go) and a woman says मैं जाती हूँ (main jaati hoon). Translators default to the masculine form. If the text is written by or for a woman, check every "I" and "my" sentence and change the verb endings — it is the correction Hindi readers notice first.

3. Write complete sentences

Fragments confuse translators because Hindi verb endings carry information English leaves to context. "Going tomorrow" could be a statement, a question or an instruction. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Hindi comes out clean: मैं कल जा रहा हूँ (main kal jaa raha hoon).

4. Keep sentences short

Hindi places the verb at the very end, so a forty-word English sentence becomes a long wait for the verb in Hindi. Split long sentences into two or three short ones before translating; the output will be dramatically more natural.

5. Use punctuation properly

Commas and full stops tell the translator where ideas end. Hindi yes–no questions often begin with क्या (kya), and the translator is far more likely to choose that structure when it can see your question mark.

6. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces very confused Hindi. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

7. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words, or transliterate them inconsistently. If a person's or company's name must stay in English letters, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards.

8. Watch English words with several meanings

"Book" the noun is किताब (kitaab), but "book a ticket" needs बुक करना (book karna). If a result looks odd, add a clarifying word in English — "book a ticket", "read a book" — and translate again.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Hindi result back into the translator in the Hindi-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a trip to India.

Travel

Essential Hindi Phrases for Travelling in India

English will carry you a long way in India, but Hindi carries you further — into the auto-rickshaw driver's good humour, the chai seller's extra biscuit, and the real price at the bazaar. Hindi is understood across most of northern and central India, and even a few phrases change how people respond to you. Here are the ones that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Start every interaction with नमस्ते (namaste), palms together if you like. Ask आप कैसे हैं? (aap kaise hain? — how are you?) and answer मैं ठीक हूँ (main theek hoon — I am fine). Thank people with धन्यवाद (dhanyavaad) or the everyday शुक्रिया (shukriya); both are always noticed.

Getting around

For auto-rickshaws and taxis, agree the price first: किराया कितना है? (kiraaya kitna hai? — how much is the fare?). On the move you will need कृपया यहाँ रोकिए (kripaya yahaan rokiye — please stop here), बाएँ मुड़िए (baayein mudiye — turn left), दाएँ मुड़िए (daayein mudiye — turn right), सीधे जाइए (seedhe jaaiye — go straight) and धीरे चलाइए (dheere chalaaiye — please drive slowly). At the station, एक टिकट दीजिए (ek ticket deejiye — one ticket, please) and ट्रेन कितने बजे है? (train kitne baje hai? — what time is the train?) cover most journeys.

Hotels and practical needs

A room is a कमरा (kamra). Ask एक कमरा चाहिए (ek kamra chaahiye — I need a room), and for the essentials: शौचालय कहाँ है? (shauchaalay kahaan hai? — where is the toilet?) and पानी मिलेगा? (paani milega? — can I get water?). The all-purpose request word is चाहिए (chaahiye — I need/want), placed after whatever you need.

Food and drink

India runs on chai. Ask एक चाय दीजिए (ek chaay deejiye — one tea, please). Declare मैं शाकाहारी हूँ (main shaakaahaari hoon — I am vegetarian) if needed, ask for less heat with कम तीखा बनाइए (kam teekha banaaiye), and praise the food with बहुत स्वादिष्ट है (bahut swaadisht hai — it is very tasty). Our food guide goes much deeper.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: मदद करो! (madad karo! — help!), डॉक्टर को बुलाइए (doctor ko bulaaiye — call a doctor) and अस्पताल कहाँ है? (aspataal kahaan hai? — where is the hospital?). India's single emergency number is 112. People in India are quick to help a traveller in trouble — often before you finish the sentence.

Numbers & Counting

Hindi Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In India you will use them for rickshaw fares, market prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and train times — often several times a day. Hindi counting has one honest difficulty: the numbers from 1 to 99 are famously irregular, each with its own name. The good news is that you only truly need the first twenty, the round tens, and the big landmarks — lakh and crore.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: एक (ek — one), दो (do — two), तीन (teen — three), चार (chaar — four), पाँच (paanch — five), छह (chhah — six), सात (saat — seven), आठ (aath — eight), नौ (nau — nine) and दस (das — ten). Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The teens and the tens

The teens run ग्यारह (gyaarah — eleven), बारह (baarah — twelve), तेरह (terah — thirteen), चौदह (chaudah — fourteen), पंद्रह (pandrah — fifteen), सोलह (solah — sixteen), सत्रह (satrah — seventeen), अठारह (athaarah — eighteen) and उन्नीस (unnees — nineteen). The round tens are बीस (bees — twenty), तीस (tees — thirty), चालीस (chaalees — forty), पचास (pachaas — fifty), साठ (saath — sixty), सत्तर (sattar — seventy), अस्सी (assi — eighty) and नब्बे (nabbe — ninety). In between, Hindi does not say "twenty-one" as a tidy compound the way English does — twenty-one is इक्कीस (ikkees), twenty-five is पच्चीस (pachchees), and each has to be met individually. Learn the ones you actually use — prices, ages, times — and let the rest arrive with practice.

Hundreds, thousands, lakhs and crores

The landmarks are regular and essential: सौ (sau — one hundred), हज़ार (hazaar — one thousand), लाख (laakh — one lakh = 100,000) and करोड़ (crore — one crore = 10,000,000). India counts big numbers in lakhs and crores, not millions: a salary of पचास लाख (pachaas laakh) is fifty lakh rupees — five million. Indian digit grouping follows the same system, so 10,00,000 is ten lakh, not one million. Knowing the word lakh prevents expensive misunderstandings.

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: यह कितने का है? (yah kitne ka hai? — how much is this?). Zero is शून्य (shoonya), which you will need for phone numbers, given digit by digit. For quantities, put the number first and add दीजिए (deejiye — please give): दो चाय दीजिए (do chaay deejiye — two teas, please) works in any tea stall in the country.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, cups of chai — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Hindi

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a train, fixing a meeting or planning around the monsoon, you need days, dates and clock times. Hindi covers it all with a small set of words — plus three special quarter-hour words that Indians use constantly and no visitor expects.

The seven days

The week runs: सोमवार (Somvaar — Monday), मंगलवार (Mangalvaar — Tuesday), बुधवार (Budhvaar — Wednesday), गुरुवार (Guruvaar — Thursday), शुक्रवार (Shukravaar — Friday), शनिवार (Shanivaar — Saturday) and रविवार (Ravivaar — Sunday). Like the English names, they honour the moon, planets and sun. The word for week is हफ़्ता (hafta), and "today" is आज (aaj).

Today, tomorrow and yesterday — one word does double duty

Here is a famous Hindi shortcut: कल (kal) means both tomorrow AND yesterday — the verb and context tell you which. कल जाऊँगा (kal jaaunga — I will go) is tomorrow; कल गया था (kal gaya tha — I had gone) is yesterday. The day after tomorrow (or before yesterday) is परसों (parson). It sounds risky, but in practice nobody ever misunderstands.

Parts of the day

The day divides into सुबह (subah — morning), दोपहर (dopahar — midday and afternoon), शाम (shaam — evening) and रात (raat — night). Greetings follow the same words — सुप्रभात (suprabhat) for good morning, शुभ संध्या (shubh sandhya) for good evening and शुभ रात्रि (shubh raatri) for good night.

Months of the year

For everyday dates, India uses the familiar month names: जनवरी (January), फ़रवरी (February), मार्च (March), अप्रैल (April), मई (May), जून (June), जुलाई (July), अगस्त (August), सितंबर (September), अक्टूबर (October), नवंबर (November) and दिसंबर (December). The traditional Hindu calendar has its own months, which set the festival dates: चैत्र (Chaitra, around March–April) opens the year, followed by वैशाख, ज्येष्ठ, आषाढ़, श्रावण (the monsoon month of Shiva), भाद्रपद, आश्विन (Dussehra and Durga Puja), कार्तिक (Diwali), मार्गशीर्ष, पौष, माघ and फाल्गुन (Holi).

Asking and telling the time

Ask कितने बजे हैं? (kitne baje hain? — what time is it?, literally "how many have struck?"). Plain hours are simple: तीन बजे (teen baje — three o'clock). Then come the three words every learner must meet. साढ़े (saadhe) means half past: साढ़े तीन (saadhe teen) is 3:30. सवा (sava) means quarter past: सवा चार (sava chaar) is 4:15. And पौने (paune) means quarter to: पौने पाँच (paune paanch) is 4:45 — "a quarter short of five". One special case: 1:30 is डेढ़ (dedh) and 2:30 is ढाई (dhaai). An hour is एक घंटा (ek ghanta) and a minute is एक मिनट (ek minat).

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the country. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family

Family Words in Hindi: Relatives and Relationships

Family sits at the centre of life in India, and Hindi has a precise word for almost every relationship — including a distinction English does not make at all: your father's family and your mother's family have completely different names. Learning these words does more than build vocabulary; it explains the structure of the Indian joint family itself.

Parents and the two sets of grandparents

Father is पिता (pita) — in everyday speech usually पिताजी (pitaji) or पापा (papa) — and mother is माँ (maa), respectfully माताजी (mataji) or मम्मी (mammi). Now the famous split: your father's father is दादा (daada) and his wife is दादी (daadi), while your mother's father is नाना (naana) and her mother is नानी (naani). Four grandparents, four unmistakable words. The general word for family is परिवार (parivaar).

Brothers and sisters

An elder brother is भैया (bhaiya) — a word also used respectfully to any young man, from shopkeepers to rickshaw drivers — and the formal word for brother is भाई (bhaai). An elder sister is दीदी (didi), used the same generous way for any older girl or woman, and sister in general is बहन (behen). Adding जी (ji) to a name or title — भाई जी, दादी जी — adds a layer of respect, and you will hear it constantly.

Uncles and aunts: every side has its own word

On your father's side: his elder brother is ताऊ (taau), his younger brother is चाचा (chaacha), and his sister is बुआ (bua). On your mother's side: her brother is मामा (maama) and her sister is मौसी (mausi). Their spouses get matching titles — चाची (chaachi), मामी (maami), मौसा (mausa). Neighbours and family friends are happily called chacha and mausi too, which is why half a Delhi street seems to be related.

Children and the next generation

A son is बेटा (beta) and a daughter is बेटी (beti) — both also used as affectionate address to any young person. A child is बच्चा (bachcha). A husband is पति (pati) and a wife is पत्नी (patni). A grandson through your son is पोता (pota), through your daughter नाती (naati) — the two-family system again, all the way down.

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is यह मेरी माँ हैं (yah meri maa hain) — notice हैं (hain), the respectful verb form used for elders even in simple sentences. "I have two sisters" is मेरी दो बहनें हैं (meri do behenen hain). Notice मेरा (mera — my, for masculine words) and मेरी (meri — my, for feminine words); both return in our self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in India, not prying — asking about someone's परिवार shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in the country.

Speaking

Hindi Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in India begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

Namaste: the greeting for everything

नमस्ते (namaste) works at any hour, for any age, in any setting — hello, good day and respectful greeting in one word. Join your palms lightly and bow your head a fraction as you say it and you have the complete Indian greeting. Its slightly more formal twin is नमस्कार (namaskaar), common in Maharashtra and in formal settings. When someone greets you first, simply return the same word with a smile.

Pranam: for elders

To grandparents, teachers and respected elders, the greeting is प्रणाम (pranaam), sometimes accompanied by bending to touch their feet — a gesture of deep respect that elders answer with a blessing, often खुश रहो (khush raho — stay happy) or जीते रहो (jeete raho — may you live long). You do not need to touch anyone's feet as a visitor; the word pranaam itself already carries the respect.

Regional greetings you will hear

In the villages and towns of Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and Rajasthan, people greet each other with राम राम (ram ram), answered by the same words. In Gujarat you will hear जय श्री कृष्णा (jai shri krishna), and in Punjab — in Punjabi rather than Hindi — सत श्री अकाल (sat shri akaal). A cheerful हैलो (hello) and हाय (hi) are perfectly normal among young people in the cities. Recognising these is enough; namaste will serve you everywhere.

Greetings by time of day

Hindi also has time-specific wishes: सुप्रभात (suprabhat — good morning), शुभ संध्या (shubh sandhya — good evening) and शुभ रात्रि (shubh raatri — good night). In casual daily life, plain namaste is more common than all three, but the morning suprabhat in a text message is a small courtesy Indians genuinely appreciate.

Asking how someone is

The standard question is आप कैसे हैं? (aap kaise hain? — how are you?, respectful). To a friend it softens to तुम कैसे हो? (tum kaise ho?). The usual answers are मैं ठीक हूँ (main theek hoon — I am fine) or सब बढ़िया (sab badhiya — all great). Asking after the family — घर पर सब कैसे हैं? (ghar par sab kaise hain? — how is everyone at home?) — is the warm follow-up everyone expects.

Welcome, congratulations and goodbye

Welcome a guest with स्वागत है (swaagat hai — welcome) or आपका स्वागत है (aapka swaagat hai). Congratulate with बधाई हो (badhaai ho — congratulations), wish a birthday with जन्मदिन मुबारक (janamdin mubaarak), and thank someone warmly with बहुत धन्यवाद (bahut dhanyavaad — thank you very much). Festival wishes get their own guide — see our festivals article.

For goodbye, the everyday word is अलविदा (alvida), but Indians more often soften parting with फिर मिलेंगे (phir milenge — we will meet again) or अपना ख्याल रखिए (apna khyaal rakhiye — take care of yourself). The idea underneath matches the greeting: nobody really leaves; they only go and return.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Hindi: Market Phrases That Work

India's bazaars — the spice lanes of Old Delhi, the silk shops of Varanasi, the weekly markets of every town — run on friendly talk. In a bazaar, the first price is an invitation to a conversation, not a final answer. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with नमस्ते (namaste) — to a shopkeeper, often भाई साहब (bhaai saahab, for a man) or दीदी (didi, for a woman). If you are just browsing, say बस देख रहा हूँ (bas dekh raha hoon — I am just looking). To ask for something specific: दिखाइए (dikhaaiye — please show me) followed by the item, or इसमें दूसरा रंग है? (ismein doosra rang hai? — do you have this in another colour?). The word for shop is दुकान (dukaan) and for market, बाज़ार (bazaar).

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: यह कितने का है? (yah kitne ka hai? — how much is this?). For things sold by weight, ask एक किलो कितने का है? (ek kilo kitne ka hai? — how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, फिर से बताइए (phir se bataaiye — please say it again) buys you a slower repeat.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: बड़ा साइज़ है? (bada size hai? — do you have a bigger size?) and छोटा साइज़ (chhota size — smaller size). To try something on: पहनकर देख सकता हूँ? (pahankar dekh sakta hoon? — may I try it on?). Common colours: लाल (laal — red), नीला (neela — blue), हरा (hara — green), काला (kaala — black), सफ़ेद (safed — white) and पीला (peela — yellow).

Bargaining: the art of mol-bhaav

Bargaining is called मोल-भाव (mol-bhaav), and in markets — less so in fixed-price shops and malls — it is expected and even enjoyed. Open with a smile: बहुत महंगा है (bahut mahanga hai — it is very expensive), then ask कुछ कम कीजिए (kuchh kam keejiye — please reduce it a little). The shopkeeper may ask your price; name one politely with इतने में दीजिए (itne mein deejiye — give it for this much). Ask आखिरी दाम क्या है? (aakhri daam kya hai? — what is your final price?) when you are close. Accept with ठीक है, यह दे दीजिए (theek hai, yah de deejiye — okay, I will take this), or decline kindly with नहीं चाहिए, धन्यवाद (nahi chaahiye, dhanyavaad — I don't want it, thank you) and walk away slowly — sometimes the best price is called after you.

Paying and finishing

Ask for the total with कुल कितना हुआ? (kul kitna hua? — how much is the total?). Cash is नकद (nakad); the change you receive is खुले पैसे (khule paise). Most shops now accept UPI payments by QR code — यूपीआई चलेगा? (UPI chalega? — will UPI work?) is a perfectly normal question anywhere in India today.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Hindi, even imperfect Hindi, changes the whole shopping experience.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Hindi

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. India has good hospitals in every city and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The words that cut through noise are मदद करो! (madad karo! — help!) and बचाओ! (bachaao! — save me / help!). Add आग! (aag! — fire!) if that is the danger, and रुकिए! (rukiye! — stop!) to halt someone urgently. India's single emergency number is 112 — it works for police, fire and ambulance across the country, and is worth saving in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: अस्पताल कहाँ है? (aspataal kahaan hai? — where is the hospital?), डॉक्टर को बुलाइए (doctor ko bulaaiye — please call a doctor), and एम्बुलेंस बुलाइए (ambulance bulaaiye — call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is डॉक्टर (doctor) and for a hospital, अस्पताल (aspataal). To say someone is injured: उसे चोट लगी है (use chot lagi hai — he/she is hurt).

Describing what is wrong

Start with मेरी तबीयत ठीक नहीं है (meri tabiyat theek nahi hai — I am not well). The word दर्द (dard — pain) combines with body parts: सिरदर्द (sirdard — headache), पेट दर्द (pet dard — stomach ache), दाँत दर्द (daant dard — toothache). Other common words: बुखार (bukhaar — fever), खाँसी (khaansi — cough), खून (khoon — blood), उल्टी (ulti — vomiting) and एलर्जी (allergy). "It hurts here" is यहाँ दर्द है (yahaan dard hai) — point as you say it.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is दवाई (dawaai), and a pharmacy is a दवाई की दुकान (dawaai ki dukaan) — look for the green cross sign. Useful lines: यह दवाई है? (yah dawaai hai? — do you have this medicine?) and दिन में कितनी बार लेनी है? (din mein kitni baar leni hai? — how many times a day should I take it?). Many Indian pharmacists speak English and are used to helping travellers match medicines by their generic names.

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: पुलिस को बुलाइए (police ko bulaaiye — call the police). "I have lost my bag" is मेरा बैग खो गया है (mera bag kho gaya hai), "my phone has been stolen" is मेरा फ़ोन चोरी हो गया है (mera phone chori ho gaya hai), and "I don't understand" — मुझे समझ नहीं आया (mujhe samajh nahi aaya) — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed. The caution word from our phrasebook, सावधान! (saavdhaan! — be careful!), is what you will hear others shout to warn you.

Grammar Basics

Hindi Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Questions are where a language starts working for you. With a handful of question words and pronouns you can ask for prices, directions, names and help anywhere in India — and understand most of the answers. This guide gives you the complete set, with examples you can use today.

The question words

  • क्या? (kya?) — What? यह क्या है? (yah kya hai? — what is this?)
  • कौन? (kaun?) — Who? वह कौन है? (vah kaun hai? — who is that?)
  • कहाँ? (kahaan?) — Where? स्टेशन कहाँ है? (station kahaan hai? — where is the station?)
  • कब? (kab?) — When? ट्रेन कब आएगी? (train kab aayegi? — when will the train come?)
  • क्यों? (kyon?) — Why? देर क्यों हुई? (der kyon hui? — why was there a delay?)
  • कैसे? (kaise?) — How? वहाँ कैसे जाएँ? (vahaan kaise jaayen? — how do we get there?)
  • कौन सा? (kaun sa?) — Which? कौन सा अच्छा है? (kaun sa achha hai? — which one is good?)
  • कितना? (kitna?) — How much? यह कितने का है? (yah kitne ka hai? — how much is this?)
  • कितने? (kitne?) — How many? कितने लोग हैं? (kitne log hain? — how many people are there?)

Notice the pattern: nearly all Hindi question words begin with the sound "k" — just as English question words begin with "wh". Learn to hear the k-words and you will catch the shape of any question.

The kya trick: turning statements into questions

The same word क्या (kya) placed at the START of a sentence turns it into a yes–no question. आप ठीक हैं (aap theek hain — you are fine) becomes क्या आप ठीक हैं? (kya aap theek hain? — are you fine?). No word order flips, no extra grammar — just add kya at the front. It is the easiest question machine in any language.

The pronouns

  • मैं (main) — I
  • तू (tu) — you (very intimate; avoid with strangers)
  • तुम (tum) — you (friendly, for friends and younger people)
  • आप (aap) — you (respectful; the safe default)
  • यह (yah) — this, he, she (nearby) · वह (vah) — that, he, she (at a distance)
  • हम (hum) — we (and, in some regions, a grand "I")
  • ये (ye) — these, they (near) · वे (ve) — those, they

Hindi pronouns carry no gender — वह (vah) means he, she or that, and the verb that follows does the clarifying. The possessives change with the gender of the thing owned, not the owner: मेरा (mera — my, before masculine words like मेरा घर, my house), मेरी (meri — my, before feminine words like मेरी किताब, my book), and respectfully आपका (aapka — your).

Putting it together

Watch how much work a few small words do: आपका नाम क्या है? (aapka naam kya hai? — what is your name?) is just "your name — what is?". Culture

Indian Festivals and Culture: Words and Wishes

India is sometimes said to have more festivals than days of the year, and each one comes with its own foods, clothes, stories and greetings. If you can wish someone well for a festival in Hindi, you will be pulled into celebrations wherever you are. Here are the biggest days of the year, what they mean, and exactly what to say.

Diwali — the festival of lights

Diwali, also called दीपावली (deepaavali — "row of lamps"), celebrates the return of Lord Rama and the victory of light over darkness. Homes glow with दीये (diye — small oil lamps), streets crackle with fireworks, and boxes of मिठाई (mithaai — sweets) travel between every house. The greeting is शुभ दीपावली (shubh deepaavali — happy Diwali) or simply दीपावली की शुभकामनाएँ (deepaavali ki shubhkaamnayen — Diwali good wishes). Expect to be offered sweets everywhere; accept at least a little, and say बहुत स्वादिष्ट है (bahut swaadisht hai — it is very tasty).

Holi — the festival of colours

Holi welcomes spring with clouds of coloured powder — गुलाल (gulaal) — and coloured water, as whole neighbourhoods turn pink, green and yellow. The night before, bonfires called होलिका दहन (holika dahan) burn away the old. Greet everyone with होली मुबारक (holi mubaarak — happy Holi), wear clothes you do not mind ruining, and try the sweet गुझिया (gujhiya) that every home prepares. Nobody stays clean, and that is entirely the point.

Eid — the feast after the fast

After the fasting month of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr is celebrated with morning prayers, new clothes, embraces and tables of food — especially the vermicelli dessert सेवइयाँ (sevaiyaan). The universal greeting, used for both Eids, is ईद मुबारक (eid mubaarak — blessed Eid). Children receive gift money called ईदी (eidi), and the correct response to any Eid greeting is to return it with a smile and both hands' warmth.

Raksha Bandhan — the thread of protection

On Raksha Bandhan, a sister ties a decorated thread — a राखी (raakhi) — on her brother's wrist, and he promises to protect her and gives a gift in return. It is one of India's most tender festivals, and in modern India friends and cousins join in freely. There is no single set greeting; a warm रक्षा बंधन की शुभकामनाएँ (raksha bandhan ki shubhkaamnayen) is always right.

Dussehra and Navratri — nine nights and a victory

The nine nights of नवरात्रि (navraatri) honour the goddess Durga with fasting, dance and — in Gujarat — swirling garba dances in mirror-work clothes. On the tenth day, दशहरा (dussehra, also called विजयादशमी, vijayaadashami), huge effigies of the demon king Ravana are burned to celebrate good's victory over evil. Wish people दशहरे की शुभकामनाएँ (dussehre ki shubhkaamnayen).

The words that work all year

Two phrases cover every festival on the calendar: शुभकामनाएँ (shubhkaamnayen — good wishes) after any festival name, and बधाई हो (badhaai ho — congratulations). A festival itself is a त्योहार (tyohaar), and a holiday is a छुट्टी (chhutti). For the everyday courtesies around festival visits — arriving, thanking, leaving — keep our greetings guide open, and check any longer message in the translator before you send it.

Speaking

How to Introduce Yourself in Hindi

A confident self-introduction is the highest-value minute of Hindi you can learn. It answers the questions every new acquaintance in India will ask — your name, your place, your work — and it turns strangers into hosts. Build yours line by line below, then say the whole thing aloud a few times.

Start with your name

Open with नमस्ते (namaste), then मेरा नाम ___ है (mera naam ___ hai — my name is ___). If someone asks आपका नाम क्या है? (aapka naam kya hai? — what is your name?), the same sentence is your answer. Names are not translated; simply place yours in the gap. If your name is hard for Hindi speakers, expect a friendly shortened version — it is a sign of acceptance, not mockery.

Say where you are from

Use मैं ___ से हूँ (main ___ se hoon — I am from ___): मैं लंदन से हूँ (main landan se hoon — I am from London). For where you live now, a man says मैं अब दिल्ली में रहता हूँ (main ab dilli mein rehta hoon — I now live in Delhi); a woman changes one word: रहती हूँ (rehti hoon). That single ending, -ta for men and -ti for women, runs through the whole language — our accuracy guide explains why it matters in translations too.

Talk about your work

The pattern is मैं एक ___ हूँ (main ek ___ hoon — I am a ___): मैं एक शिक्षक हूँ (main ek shikshak hoon — I am a teacher), मैं एक डॉक्टर हूँ (main ek doctor hoon — I am a doctor), मैं एक इंजीनियर हूँ (main ek engineer hoon — I am an engineer). A student says मैं एक छात्र हूँ (main ek chhaatra hoon) or, for a woman, छात्रा (chhaatra). Not sure of your job's Hindi name? Check it in the translator — professions translate cleanly, and many are simply English words in Hindi dress.

Mention your family

Family talk is welcome talk here. "I have one elder sister" is मेरी एक बड़ी बहन है (meri ek badi behen hai). Married speakers can add मैं शादीशुदा हूँ (main shaadishuda hoon — I am married). The full family vocabulary — including the famous two sets of grandparents — lives in our family guide.

Add your languages — and a humble line

Two honest lines that always win smiles: a man says मैं हिंदी सीख रहा हूँ (main hindi seekh raha hoon — I am learning Hindi; a woman says सीख रही हूँ, seekh rahi hoon) and मुझे थोड़ी हिंदी आती है (mujhe thodi hindi aati hai — I know a little Hindi). People will slow down, help you, and often teach you a word on the spot.

Close politely

Finish with आपसे मिलकर खुशी हुई (aapse milkar khushi hui — nice to meet you) and धन्यवाद (dhanyavaad — thank you). That is a complete, respectful introduction in under a minute. Practise yours with the phrases in the phrasebook, and you are ready for any first meeting in India.

Food & Drink

Hindi for Food Lovers: Ordering Food in India

Food is India's love language, and even a little Hindi transforms eating here — waiters light up, portions grow, and the best off-menu items appear. This guide covers the words for the dishes you will meet and the phrases that get them made your way.

Breads, rice and the thali

The daily breads are रोटी (roti — flat whole-wheat bread), its puffed cousin फुल्का (phulka), the layered पराठा (paraatha) and the tandoor-baked नान (naan). Rice is चावल (chaaval), and the lentil dish that accompanies everything is दाल (daal). A complete meal served together on one plate is a थाली (thaali) — the best first order in any new town, because it lets you taste six dishes at once. Add सब्ज़ी (sabzi — a vegetable dish), पनीर (paneer — fresh cheese) and रायता (raaita — cooling yoghurt) and you can read most menus in the country.

Street snacks: the chaat family

India's streets run on snacks: समोसा (samosa — the famous spiced-potato triangle), पकौड़ा (pakauda — fritters, perfect in the rain), कचौड़ी (kachaudi — a flaky, spicy disc) and the whole tangy चाट (chaat) family — try गोलगप्पे (golgappe, called paani puri in Mumbai): crisp shells filled with spiced water that you eat in one brave bite. Order with एक प्लेट समोसा दीजिए (ek plate samosa deejiye — one plate of samosa, please).

Biryani and the celebratory dishes

For a feast, the word is बिरयानी (biryaani) — fragrant layered rice, with Hyderabadi and Lucknowi styles as the great rivals. Around it gather कबाब (kabaab), butter chicken in the north, and the slow-cooked राजमा (raajma — kidney-bean curry) and छोले (chhole — chickpea curry) of home cooking. If a family invites you to eat, the correct response to everything is a second helping.

Chai: more than a drink

The day's rhythm is set by चाय (chaay) — strong black tea boiled with milk, sugar and spices (मसाला चाय, masaala chaay), served in a glass or an earthen cup called a कुल्हड़ (kulhad). Order एक चाय, कम मीठी (ek chaay, kam meethi — one tea, less sweet). Refusing chai is almost impossible and rarely wise: it is how conversations, deals and friendships begin.

Sweets: mithaas

Save room. The spiral syrupy जलेबी (jalebi) is fried to order and eaten hot; गुलाब जामुन (gulaab jaamun) are soft milk dumplings in syrup; रसगुल्ला (rasgulla) are spongy and light; and a box of mixed मिठाई (mithaai — sweets) is the standard gift for any visit. During festivals, sweet shops stay open half the night — see our festivals guide for when to go.

Making it your way

The control phrases: कम तीखा (kam teekha — less spicy), कम नमक (kam namak — less salt), शाकाहारी (shaakaahaari — vegetarian; India marks veg food with a green dot and non-veg with a red one) and और दीजिए (aur deejiye — please give more). Praise with बहुत स्वादिष्ट है! (bahut swaadisht hai! — it is very tasty!) and settle up with बिल दीजिए (bill deejiye — please give the bill).

Grammar Basics

How Hindi Sentences Are Built: Word Order and Verb Endings

Hindi grammar sounds complicated from the outside, but its sentences are built from a few steady habits. Learn these habits and you can both decode translations from this site and start assembling your own sentences with confidence.

Habit one: the verb comes last

English order is subject–verb–object: "She reads a book." Hindi order is subject–object–verb: वह किताब पढ़ती है (vah kitaab padhti hai — literally "she book reads"). When you check a translation, read it backwards from the verb and the sentence suddenly makes sense. Time words like आज (aaj — today) usually sit early, and the person being spoken to or about takes the postpositions explained next.

Habit two: little words come after, not before

English uses prepositions — in, on, to, from, of. Hindi uses postpositions, glued after the noun: घर में (ghar mein — in the house), मेज़ पर (mez par — on the table), दिल्ली से (dilli se — from Delhi), दोस्त के साथ (dost ke saath — with a friend). The word को (ko) marks the person or thing receiving the action: उसे बुलाओ (use bulaao — call him/her). And the possessive का (ka) changes with the gender of the thing possessed: राम का घर (Ram ka ghar — Ram's house), राम की किताब (Ram ki kitaab — Ram's book).

Habit three: the verb ending tells you who — and their gender

Hindi verbs change their tails to show person, tense and gender. Take "go": a man says मैं जाता हूँ (main jaata hoon — I go), a woman says मैं जाती हूँ (main jaati hoon). For "you" the ending follows the respect level from our pronouns guide: तुम जाते हो (tum jaate ho) to a friend, आप जाते हैं (aap jaate hain) with respect. The helper verb at the very end — हूँ, है, हैं, हो — carries the tense, and swapping it for था (tha — was) moves the whole sentence into the past.

Habit four: questions start with kya

As the questions guide shows, a statement becomes a yes–no question by adding क्या (kya) at the front: आप दिल्ली जाएँगे (aap dilli jaayenge — you will go to Delhi) → क्या आप दिल्ली जाएँगे? (will you go to Delhi?). Question words like कहाँ (kahaan — where) slot into the sentence where the answer would go, without flipping the word order around as English does.

Habit five: negatives and no articles

To make a sentence negative, put नहीं (nahi) just before the verb: मैं नहीं जाऊँगा (main nahi jaaunga — I will not go). And two English habits you can simply drop: Hindi has no words for "a" or "the" — किताब (kitaab) alone means a book or the book, as context requires — and in the present tense, short sentences can even drop the verb "is": यह मेरा घर (yah mera ghar) already means "this is my house". Plurals are usually shown by the verb and context rather than by changing every noun.

Build your first sentences

Try the pattern yourself: pick a pronoun from the pronouns list, an object from the vocabulary guide, and a verb — then check your sentence in the translator. Example: मैं हिंदी पढ़ रहा हूँ (main hindi padh raha hoon — I am studying Hindi). If the translator agrees with you, you are no longer memorising Hindi — you are building it.
